This is my write-up of episode 9 of This Week in Open Lakehouse, the weekly show Scott Haines and I host on open source data engineering news. It's a long one (we recorded on a Friday, ran well past our usual length, and still dropped things for time), so if you'd rather skim than listen I understand completely; the video is above, and the audio, show notes and full transcript are further down this page.

We opened on the holidays, which in my line of work mark the start of the slow period, and I wondered aloud whether releases work the same way (everyone cramming code in before they disappear to enjoy their lives). Scott's prediction was the opposite: the humans can go see family, but the agents keep working, so he doesn't expect the cadence to slow at all. I admitted that my agents get a little downtime of their own; on my server they pull work from a quest list, and after each finished quest they get a few turns on a shared cellular automata board, which grows more complicated with every task (they don't care about it, I'm fairly sure, and I anthropomorphize them anyway). The joke underneath all of this is that the week proved Scott right before we'd finished saying it, since nearly every project we follow shipped something in the same few days.

**What struck me reading the release notes afterwards is how little of that week was new capability and how much was tightening (catalogs narrowing what a privilege allows, Kafka and Airflow closing CVEs, Spark switching on stricter security defaults, Comet spending a release making its answers match Spark's, Parquet arguing over who must enforce a vector's contract), because the same agents that keep the release train running through the holidays also make holes cheap to find; the result is that the old version you're sitting on has quietly become the one with the documented problems rather than the safe, known quantity.**

## Parquet's vector type, finitely

We've followed the Parquet vector discussion for weeks, and this week it became a vote. The [FINITE_VECTOR proposal](https://github.com/apache/parquet-format/pull/624) is a logical type on top of the canonical three-level LIST structure rather than a new physical representation: every non-null vector has exactly `num_elements` elements, the elements are required and finite (no null, NaN, +Inf or -Inf), and they're numeric or boolean primitives, while a reader that doesn't know the annotation just reads an ordinary list. The name is the design decision; it was VECTOR until the community renamed it to state its contract, leaving plain VECTOR free for a more general type (one that tolerates the NaNs and infinities everyone argued about) or a dedicated physical layout later. I said on the show that splitting off the constrained case first is the open source way to do this, and I still think so: ship the small thing whose behavior you can control, watch it, then widen it.

One correction to what we said. I described the [vote](https://lists.apache.org/thread/fm2373y1d0pk20x6rw8jdkl6kwvfoc32) as likely to pass despite one binding -1, and the full story is better than that. The -1 came from Antoine Pitrou, and it was about one sentence, "Writers must enforce these rules"; Parquet doesn't require writers to validate anything else (a STRING column is only something that *should* be UTF-8, and Parquet C++ doesn't check), so making FINITE_VECTOR the one type whose writers must scan for NaNs would change the spec's philosophy, not just one type. The PR was revised, he moved to +1, and the [vote passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/rc4dso5jd5qqbf1b6cxcw88ld70l52l3) on November 9 with 7 binding +1s and no -1s, three days after we recorded. That's what Scott meant about a dev list full of senior engineers earning its keep; a format used nearly everywhere gets one chance to decide who is responsible for an invariant, and arguing about it before the type ships is far cheaper than after.

The type is already spreading. Iceberg has an open [spec PR adding a vector type to v4](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/18416), and on Spark, a second correction: we said there was an SPIP ticket, but what exists is Huaxin Gao's [message to the dev list](https://lists.apache.org/thread/tr04707snmmmg9dwcqq4z81lrjj72mv8) on November 4 asking whether anyone was already working on a VECTOR type and offering to send a SPIP if not. Scott's point about the order of operations holds either way; the format settles the bytes first so every engine and table format reads the same type the same way.

## Spark 4.3, and what's now on by default

Spark 4.3.0 was [announced on November 5](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jp456tktqwdns6gs5jv506b08c6n4f1t), and the [release notes](https://spark.apache.org/releases/spark-release-4-3-0.html) are long enough that Scott had to slow me down. The headline is stateful real-time mode, with a correction on lineage: real-time mode arrived in Spark 4.1 (not 4.2, as I said) for stateless Scala queries, and 4.3 adds streaming aggregation, `dropDuplicates` and `transformWithState` on pipelined shuffles, drops the `@Experimental` annotation, and puts end-to-end latency "on the order of 100 milliseconds." Scott, who remembers continuous processing sitting in experimental for the whole 2.x line, read the quick graduation as a measure of the stabilization that went in; stateful is the hard part, because updating distributed state one event at a time still has to roll back and restart on another cluster as though nothing happened. My guess is that a lot of teams running Spark next to Flink will now try running only Spark.

The small-data work comes with a correction. I called it Project Feather and said it kicks in on its own; the umbrella is the SPIP for faster queries in local laptop mode (SPARK-56978), and what landed in 4.3 is a shuffle-free single-task execution rule for small, single-partition fragments (`spark.sql.optimizer.singleTaskExecution.enabled`) plus an Arrow-native cache serializer, both opt-in and off by default. Once you enable the rule the optimizer picks which fragments qualify, but I couldn't find the 100 MB threshold I mentioned anywhere in the source. The point stands that there's no reason to shuffle when there's nowhere to shuffle to, and it makes Spark a more reasonable thing to prototype on (or point an agent at) before you need a cluster.

| Area | What's new in 4.3.0 | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Declarative Pipelines | SCD Type 2 in AUTO CDC (`stored_as_scd_type=2`, `STORED AS SCD TYPE 2`), history as rows bounded by `__START_AT` and `__END_AT` | Available |
| SQL | `ASOF JOIN ... MATCH_CONDITION`, `JSON_TABLE`, ANSI `UNNEST`, `@` time travel on table names, `jaro_winkler_similarity`, `counter_diff` | ASOF join off by default |
| VARIANT | `variant_set`, `variant_insert`, `variant_delete`; extraction pushdown through aggregates, sorts and joins | Available |
| Types | Nanosecond `TIMESTAMP_NTZ(p)` / `TIMESTAMP_LTZ(p)`, p from 7 to 9 | Preview, off by default |
| DSv2 | Generated columns on V2 writes, runtime filtering interface for scans | Available |
| Python | Python 3.10 dropped; experimental UDF-to-expression transpiler (Holden Karau's work); `DataFrame.zip` | Transpiler opt-in |
| Connect | REST API for sessions and operations; JDBC driver returns `ARRAY`, `MAP`, `STRUCT` | Available |
| Security | UI content security policy, `reflect` allowlist, restricted unpickler in the Python client, opt-in restricted SQL mode | CSP on by default |

I can't stress AUTO CDC enough; with Type 2 you get full history from a declarative flow instead of a hand-written merge, which is where CDC pipelines usually go wrong. Scott's pick was the variant mutation functions, which let a team stabilize a messy, still-evolving variant column without rewriting the pipeline, and he caught one I'd skipped that matters for upgrades: Python 3.11 is now the oldest version PySpark supports. For DSv2, the interview I did with Szehon Ho on the Spark YouTube channel is the long version.

A correction on the release candidate, too. I said it stopped and started mostly for security hardening; the vote threads show RC2 drew objections over regressions (a write failure on GCS and ABFS, an Arrow Python UDF failure on Connect), and [RC3 passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/631wfyhswlz9361pm3y466g04f7lk6sm) on October 30. The security hardening is in the release itself, in the table above.

Looking ahead, the [External Lookup Join SPIP passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/678vpc4x475sjsn2lmfhwbdd4r0cxrjc), Jing Zhan opened a [SPIP to extend DSv2 push-down](https://lists.apache.org/thread/t7y7ooz680olqldoh2lb321n956ht2h5) to more join types, set operations and window functions, and a [SPIP for a built-in ADBC data source](https://lists.apache.org/thread/vz0s8nok4mgyfmcz1wmoc9ko14xkts9o) would read Arrow record batches instead of JDBC rows (its prototype measured 3 to 10x faster reads from columnar databases). On Java I was imprecise: [master now requires Java 21](https://lists.apache.org/thread/krnx29qzh19019sfk0hrp6k1bp18s91c), so Spark 5.0.0 drops 17 and builds on 21 or 25 (25 was proposed as the minimum and not adopted), while the 3.5 and 4.x branches keep their Java support.

## The catalogs learn to say no

Every catalog we covered spent the week narrowing what a grant lets you do. [Lakekeeper 0.15.0](https://github.com/lakekeeper/lakekeeper/releases/tag/v0.15.0) adds a `write` privilege under OpenFGA that lets a pipeline or dbt model change a table's data, schema and snapshots (including `CREATE OR REPLACE`) without being able to drop or rename it, which stays with `modify`. Scott framed it as the line between DML and control; you want people modifying and reading tables, not dropping one a downstream team depends on. [PR 2249](https://github.com/lakekeeper/lakekeeper/pull/2249) also makes vended storage credentials match what the caller may do (read gets read-only, write without read gets nothing), and the release lists several of these as breaking changes, which is the honest way to ship them.

[Unity Catalog](https://github.com/unitycatalog/unitycatalog/pull/2023), on main toward 0.8.0, turns on stricter privilege checks by default: grants on invalid object types are rejected, read access is checked before Iceberg credentials are returned, `MODIFY` no longer counts for metadata checks, and renaming a volume or model needs schema creation rights, with new privileges such as `READ METADATA` and `WRITE VOLUME`. In [Polaris](https://github.com/apache/polaris/pull/5263) the fix is the opposite of the rollback we described on air. A failed commit used to delete its newly written `metadata.json` on any error, including opaque database failures where the catalog pointer may already have moved (leaving the catalog pointing at a deleted file); now Polaris cleans up only on known failures and otherwise reports the commit state as unknown, because when you can't prove a commit failed, deleting its files is how you corrupt the table.

## Iceberg v4, and the Rust side of the table

[Default value expressions](https://lists.apache.org/thread/h5jnk7l2op325x545hfnrpbo3qmh2ovo) passed for v4 with 11 binding +1s the morning we recorded; a write default can now be an expression such as the current timestamp, evaluated when the row is written, using constants and function calls but not other fields. [Stats-only fields](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/18405) merged into the spec on November 2 (I said a vote passed over Halloween; the merge is what I can confirm), letting a table keep statistics on derived values it doesn't store, like a `lower()` expression, so an engine can prune files for a case-insensitive filter without adding a column.

[Iceberg Rust 0.11.0](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-rust/releases/tag/v0.11.0) finishes encrypted writes, applies v3 deletion vectors on read, and introduces an `AuthManager` design for REST catalogs as a breaking change; it's pre-1.0, so production is on you. It's also the last line in which Iceberg governs `iceberg-datafusion`, which now lives in [apache/datafusion-iceberg](https://github.com/apache/datafusion-iceberg). A correction to Scott's Cargo note: after a naming detour the crate [keeps its name](https://github.com/apache/datafusion-iceberg/issues/30), so for most people it's a version bump to the DataFusion-governed 0.12 line rather than a new dependency. Elsewhere in Rust, [hudi-rs 0.6.0](https://github.com/apache/hudi-rs/releases/tag/release-0.6.0) adds experimental merge-on-read and metadata table compaction (experimental meaning, as Scott put it, the contracts may still change), IN and NOT IN file pruning and record-index lookups, and [Paimon Mosaic 0.3.0](https://lists.apache.org/thread/3k595cyvgzyv446h3sx5sbbs3nld68cy), the columnar-bucket format for [tables of 10,000 or more columns](https://paimon.apache.org/docs/mosaic/), adds command-line tools and stricter writer schema validation.

## Two accelerators under one Spark

[DataFusion Comet 1.2.0](https://datafusion.apache.org/blog/2026/11/02/datafusion-comet-1.2.0) is, by its own description, largely a release about returning the same answer as Spark (timestamp labels, -0.0 and NaN, collations, decimal averages, nested types, join order) and about surviving memory pressure, which is where accelerators lose trust. Native plans hand memory back before the task ends, failed allocations retry instead of failing the task, and JVM shuffle with many partitions no longer gives every partition a full 32 to 64 MB page on its first row, which took the project's TPC-H SF2 repartition from 266 seconds to 1.1. Native Iceberg writes (v3 tables included) are documented and opt-in via `spark.comet.write.iceberg.enabled`, Delta Lake scans and Arrow Python UDFs arrive as experimental, and Spark 4.2 is now supported. I wasn't sure on the show about the Arrow in-memory cache: it stays opt-in, because auditing it turned up default settings that could return rows in the wrong order, and I find that choice more reassuring than any benchmark in the post. Comet 1.1.0 [shipped in early October](https://datafusion.apache.org/blog/2026/10/01/datafusion-comet-1.1.0), so as Scott said, that's a lot for about a month, and our running thanks to Andy Grove continue.

NVIDIA's [cuDF plugin for Apache Spark](https://nvidia.github.io/cudf-spark/docs/download.html), formerly the RAPIDS Accelerator (old `spark-rapids` links redirect, artifact names unchanged for now), shipped 26.10.0, our first time covering it. It adds GPU variant extraction, basic Delta 4.2 and 4.3 compatibility with Change Data Feed reads, GPU `REORG TABLE ... APPLY (PURGE)` and DML writes to deletion-vector tables, and Iceberg v3 deletion vector reads and writes, for Spark up to 4.2.0 (I'm curious whether my one consumer NVIDIA card and Ubuntu box can run it). Scott's observation is the one to keep: deletion vectors make every merge-on-read table pay for its deletes in joins at read time, joins are what GPUs parallelize well, so accelerating both the reads and the purge accelerates the maintenance bill rather than the headline query.

## Airflow 3.4, Kafka's JWT, and Flink on Ray

[Airflow 3.4.0](https://airflow.apache.org/docs/apache-airflow/3.4.0/release_notes.html) landed minutes before we recorded. Task loops (AIP-111) let a task group repeat a runtime-decided number of times, do-while style with an `until` condition and `max_iterations` cap, each iteration seeing the last one's result (Scott's point: that's an agent loop). The Dag Importer (AIP-85) parses TypeScript, Go and Java SDK Dags and native YAML, all experimental in 3.4. Psycopg 3 becomes the default Postgres driver, with statement preparation disabled so it works behind transaction-mode PgBouncer (a connection pooler, correcting Scott's on-air guess that it was an auth layer), Python 3.10 is gone as in Spark, and the images move to Docker Hardened Images. Three days later Airflow disclosed [CVE-2026-50204](https://lists.apache.org/thread/9pcjt268j7yb2mp77pwghjrf63r6tybf), an audit log that kept connection and variable secrets in cleartext, fixed only from 3.4.0.

Kafka gets a correction. The bug I mentioned, [CVE-2026-110985](https://lists.apache.org/thread/7wbocdvn1yhg8zh7hxsd532dr7z160zy), is a broker using SASL/OAUTHBEARER *without* a JWKS endpoint URL (not with one) silently falling back to a validator that checks no signature at all, so it accepted forged tokens for any principal; from 4.2.2, 4.3.1 and 4.4.0 such a broker refuses to start. ([Kafka 4.4.0](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jfjmszz4tx3s7tkhsf943yfx2x7f8brf) was announced October 26, not October 6.) Scott's advice was to always check the signature even when skipping it saves a few milliseconds, and his larger point is this post's thesis; plenty of these bugs probably sat exploitable for years, and what changed is that finding them no longer requires the one person you knew who could reliably write the exploit.

In Flink, [FLIP-608](https://lists.apache.org/thread/j5nr4yfj6x1s0sxx756dd673g5bwc94v) (a Ray resource backend) was accepted with 8 binding +1s, [Flink Agents 0.4.0](https://lists.apache.org/thread/lch3tnldch6qssm2x70f0wfb6t829cmw) shipped as a preview not meant for production, and a [GEOGRAPHY type for Flink SQL](https://lists.apache.org/thread/fz78r7xlb0btykmxgwdz4cq7zox8vnpn) is under discussion.

## Update hygiene, while the agents work through the holidays

My sign-off was a plea for update hygiene. Plenty of people I talk to in the Spark ecosystem are still on 3.2 or 3.5 with a bespoke solution built around the gap, and it's never as good as what's now in the official distribution, because far more people maintain and validate that one. Scott supplied the practical half: agents can do much of a migration's heavy lifting now, so start with low-risk pipelines, learn the patterns, work toward the critical ones, and don't skip the 4.x line.

This week makes that advice sharper than it sounds. Scott was right in the cold open that the agents won't take the holidays off, and the changelogs show what they're doing with the time; they're helping projects close their own holes (a catalog that deleted the wrong file, a broker that trusted any token, an audit log that kept secrets in cleartext) at the same pace they help ship features. Each of those fixes is also a public description of a problem in the version before it, which is why staying put stopped being the conservative choice, and the same agents that wrote the fix can do most of the upgrade.
