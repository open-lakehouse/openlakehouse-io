This is my write-up of episode 4 of This Week in Open Lakehouse, the weekly show Scott Haines and I host on open source data engineering news; the video is above, and the audio, show notes and full transcript are further down this page. We recorded it in week 40 of the year (October 2nd, for those of you who are normal like me) and opened on that chart that draws your whole life as a grid of weekly squares, which should thrill someone who works in data and mostly made us both a little existential.

The tangent I actually want to start from came a few minutes later. I'd been trying to test the Spark 4.3 release candidate with Iceberg and found that the Iceberg Spark runtime was still on Spark 4.1 (no 4.2 build at all), and that reminded me of something from my time as a research assistant in a sleep and circadian rhythms lab. The roughly 24-hour rhythm your body expresses is the average of the individual cycles of clock cells in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and it only holds while those cells stay in concert with each other; as they drift apart with age, the expressed rhythm weakens, which is a large part of why sleep gets harder as you get older. I offered it on the show as a very weird analogy for data infrastructure, and I think it holds up better than I expected. Every project in the stack now keeps its own release clock, AI is speeding all of those clocks up, and the stack you run only works where they stay in phase.

**That was the through-line of the week: almost everything that moved was a contract at the boundary between two independently released pieces (a catalog's policy that the engine has to enforce, an identity token that executors should only ever see the consequences of, a gateway that lets a client stop caring which server it talks to, a schema that can be read without the library that wrote it), because once no project will slow down to wait for the others, an explicit contract at each seam, and the maintainers cutting releases on several branches at once, is the only thing that keeps the whole stack in sync.**

## delta-rs 1.0, and the stack it had to wait for

Scott kept saying delta-rs was about to hit 1.0 in earlier episodes, and this time it actually did; [rust-v1.0.0](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/releases/tag/rust-v1.0.0) went out on September 28 with three changes he walked through. The first is that the `S3DynamoDbLogStore` is gone. Delta needs an atomic "put if absent" to make concurrent commits safe, and for years S3 couldn't provide one, so delta-rs coordinated writers through a DynamoDB table instead; now that S3 has conditional writes, delta-rs [uses those by default](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/discussions/4482) and the extra table goes away (which also means coordinating with Spark writers through the old DynamoDB setup no longer works). The second is the upgrade to DataFusion 55, which Scott framed as a reverse trickle: Arrow ships, then DataFusion moves to it, then delta-rs can move to DataFusion. One correction to the version chain we gave on the show: delta-rs 1.0 is built on Arrow 59, not Arrow 60, which hadn't been announced yet when it shipped. DataFusion also stays behind a Cargo feature, so a project that never queries through it doesn't compile it in (Scott's case for small binaries is containers that bootstrap fast).

The third change is column mapping, which is the one I'd upgrade for. Without it, a reader that resolves columns by position breaks the moment someone reorders or drops a column; Scott's example was swapping a name column and an age column, and a consumer asking for a string and getting numbers back. Column mapping gives every column a stable physical name separate from its logical one, so a rename or reorder is a metadata change rather than a rewrite or a broken downstream job. The 1.0 milestone was really three separately released projects finally lining up at once.

## Iceberg 1.12: the house is framed, and variant got fast

[Iceberg 1.12.0](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/releases/tag/apache-iceberg-1.12.0) passed its vote on September 30 with Neelesh Salian as release manager, after an extra release candidate that came down to exactly the kind of coordination problem we were talking about (the staging script still listed Spark 3.5, 4.0 and 4.1 even though Spark 4.2 support had landed in the source, and on the [RC2 thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/491c0065os0fsnsqny9vjhf0g4hg4o2y) the open question became whether 1.12 was meant to ship 4.2 binaries at all). It didn't; there is still no `iceberg-spark-runtime-4.2` artifact on Maven Central, which is why my test was stuck on 4.1. Scott wrote it all up in [A Field Guide to Apache Iceberg 1.12](/blog/iceberg/iceberg-1-12-field-guide), and his analogy is the framing of a house: a lot of V4 internals are visible in this release (the unified manifest entry, typed content stats, relative paths), but the adaptive metadata tree that replaces manifest lists, and the scan and commit paths that use it, aren't there yet, so nothing has a permit and you shouldn't set `format-version` to 4.

If you don't care about V4, you probably care about variant. Before 1.12, a single variant column forced the entire table into row-at-a-time Parquet reads; 1.12 adds [vectorized reads for variant columns](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/16292), on by default for unshredded variant, with shredded files falling back to row reads until that path follows (Scott expects 1.13). Scott admitted he used to argue against variant entirely; his case for it now is that a value can keep changing shape (his example was a record of what's in my computer, which changes every time I swap a GPU) without breaking the schema, while staying binary and cheap to traverse. The practical advice from both of us was to try unshredded variant on 1.12 and shred later. 1.12 also wires an [eager fetch into the Parquet read path](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/17284), so any Parquet file of 1 MB or less is read in a single request instead of three, which is a direct win for small files and manifests.

The item that matters most for this week's theme is the [expressions spec](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/16652), written by Ryan Blue, which gives Iceberg one recursive grammar for filters and expressions so that any engine can serialize a predicate and every other engine reaches the same answer (Scott's version: a sum should always be the same sum). One correction to what we said: Scott described it as baselined but not officially part of the REST catalog yet, and it's a little further along than that, since the [REST OpenAPI was updated](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/17138) to the new forms in 1.12. What's still missing is implementations; a [thread on the dev list](https://lists.apache.org/thread/48pqzgp1x095yn7clrpbh1vb56gfbq1t) points out that the Java, Go and C++ libraries still only read and write the deprecated form, and asks how servers should behave while clients migrate. Anoop Johnson has already [volunteered to manage 1.13](https://lists.apache.org/thread/mj8czb2ljf8kj1d7ldbfw39sffr9knjc), aiming for another release before the end of the year.

## Spark security: who enforces the policy, and who holds the token

The Spark half of the episode was three proposals that all answer the same question, which is what the engine owes a catalog, an identity provider or a client that it doesn't control.

Huaxin Gao's [SPIP for pluggable row-filter and column-mask enforcement](https://lists.apache.org/thread/bfdvfq7576w64kmjhhtxf16xwd3rx041) starts from an uncomfortable fact: Spark has no supported API for a table or catalog to declare a read policy, so integrators (Apache Ranger through Kyuubi's AuthZ plugin, and a recent Iceberg proof of concept) inject Catalyst rules through `SparkSessionExtensions`, which is brittle across versions and, worse, unsafe, since a masking projection added naively can be removed or collapsed by the optimizer and silently return unmasked data. The proposal adds a `SupportsAccessControl` interface enforced during analysis with three guarantees: masks can't be optimized away, the row filter always sees the original pre-mask values, and anything Spark can't resolve fails the read instead of returning unprotected rows. This is the other half of something Iceberg merged this summer, a spec for [read restrictions returned with `loadTable`](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/13879), where the catalog hands back projections and row filters (expressed, conveniently, in that new expressions spec) and expects a trusted engine to apply them. My point on the show was that catalog policies the engine doesn't enforce are one-sided governance, and Scott's question was where the trust boundary sits; he suspects it makes the most sense behind Spark Connect, where the server applies the policy and the client never sees the difference, though that only holds if engineers can't reach the server side directly.

The second proposal is [OIDC credential propagation](https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/SPARK-57703). Spark's credential story was built around Hadoop and Kerberos, while most deployments now run on Kubernetes against object storage with OIDC tokens; the design has the driver read the workload's OIDC token, exchange it for short-lived storage credentials through a pluggable `CredentialProvider`, push those credentials to executors over a new RPC, and refresh them before they expire, mirroring how delegation tokens already work on YARN. It's off by default, the core SPI stays generic, and the AWS STS implementation lives in its own `connector/credential-aws` module. One correction to what I said: I described this as something Spark is currently looking into, and it's well past that. The [vote passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/qprfz03mqscjq0o09nkkosv4fffhod3z) back in June and the work has been merging since, though it was [pulled from branch-4.3](https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/SPARK-58756) and now targets 4.4.0. My favorite part of the proposal is its argument that plenty of data access never passes through a catalog at all (landing zones, joins against path-based files, custom connectors), so Spark doesn't need to become the authorization system; it only needs to make sure that whatever credential gets vended reaches the right executors and nowhere else. Scott's angle was agents: with ephemeral clusters spun up per task, an identity handshake at the driver is also a natural way to quarantine what each one can touch.

The third is the [Spark Connect Gateway](https://lists.apache.org/thread/7s3vdw92vjo0sl72rbbwgo8fyck295z3), proposed by Liang-Chi Hsieh and [approved in August](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jtys2ph4drqgwqwr3j6bl8sg31mjn0ko). A Connect client has to stick to one server for its whole session because the session state lives in that driver's memory, and a generic load balancer can't help because the routing key sits inside the request protobuf, so every team running more than one Connect server writes its own proxy. The gateway is a [Rust gRPC proxy](https://github.com/apache/spark-connect-gateway) that pins each session to a backend and adds OIDC and JWT auth, per-tenant routing and rate limits, audit logs and tracing, while the client only changes the address it connects to; the trade-off is that the backends become, by design, hard to reach any other way. Scott got excited about moving a job to a larger cluster when it hits a spike that adaptive query execution can't absorb, or sending GPU stages to one cluster and CPU stages to another. I'd add a caveat there: as designed, the gateway pins a session to one backend precisely because the state can't move, so that kind of mid-flight rerouting is a future question about what a Spark application even is once one client can talk to several clusters, which Scott flagged too.

What makes the gateway workable is the observability that shipped in 4.3: Spark Connect now [exposes operation IDs for end-to-end request attribution](https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/SPARK-58547), so with many clients and many servers you can trace which client sent which long-running query, and the gateway uses operation IDs to route reattach and interrupt calls back to the server that owns the operation. Scott compared it to request IDs in OpenTelemetry; you can't govern a distributed system you can't trace.

## Arrow schemas you can read without Arrow

The last proposal was Arrow's [JSON representation of Arrow schemas](https://lists.apache.org/thread/wdb9g2k55rqtd7jf5ogn93b3pm6om1b5), started by Kent Wu. Arrow schemas today only serialize canonically as FlatBuffers inside IPC, which is fine inside an Arrow pipeline and awkward everywhere else (JSON APIs, schemas in config files and version control, ADBC's upcoming metadata APIs, which motivated the proposal). I wasn't sure on the show what Arrow calls its improvement proposals; it doesn't really have a named process like Airflow's AIPs, and this one is a format discussion on the dev list with a design doc attached. I also wondered why they didn't use FlatBuffers' own JSON form, and the doc answers it: FlatBuffers JSON is only supported in a handful of languages, which defeats the point of cross-language interchange. YAML and TOML were rejected for their ambiguities (YAML's implicit type coercion, TOML's limited nesting), and the format carries its own semantic version, independent of Arrow's, with a conformance suite certifying that a reader and writer pair round-trips losslessly.

Scott's take was that this is how standardization usually happens; several projects carry an Arrow schema their own way (Flight SQL sends one alongside its buffers), a best practice emerges, and once it's written back into the project it becomes the practice. JSON is the boring and correct choice because every engine we talked about already reads it, and when DuckDB, DataFusion, Polars and Spark each ship Arrow across the wire their own way, a schema anyone can parse is one less translator to maintain. On the release side, [Arrow Rust 60.0.0](https://arrow.apache.org/blog/2026/09/29/arrow-rs-60.0.0/) went out on September 29, which starts the trickle over again for everything built on top of it.

## The release managers keep the clocks in phase

I wanted to end on the part nobody writes blog posts about. At the time we recorded, Spark had three release candidate votes open at once ([4.2.1](https://lists.apache.org/thread/bl3zbvbmrhz2m6jz25ogfwfd6v3wjo4q), [4.1.4](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jr3gfsv3k2qq8f24w13rx3s44jm2j5r4) and [4.0.5](https://lists.apache.org/thread/ov7hfrb2jx17lt5xgs0zjqpmjotkm9bb)), with 4.3.0 waiting on blockers after its first candidate. One correction to my list on the show: I mentioned 3.4.1 needing patches, and there was no 3.4 release in flight (the 3.5 line is the long-term one, and [3.5.10](https://lists.apache.org/thread/x5dv5wb85olw3d2c0x7x959dh0qsy6kx) only came up for discussion at the end of October). The three maintenance releases needed several more candidates each before [4.2.1](https://lists.apache.org/thread/nthkx17msj76fy6bofrl1xmkz4b0493v), [4.1.4](https://lists.apache.org/thread/dptwhbfnkwtxv3svx5o6t7pql25p9bow) and [4.0.5](https://lists.apache.org/thread/6h87roy79r6jdhx6stfvs4tw54w0zf18) passed on October 27, and [4.3.0 was announced](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jp456tktqwdns6gs5jv506b08c6n4f1t) on November 5. Every new minor release adds another branch someone has to backport to, and Scott's point is that the pressure is only going up, because AI is now picking apart decades of human decisions and turning up vulnerabilities that only a tiny fraction of people could have found before. Hyukjin Kwon is meanwhile [trialing automated AI review](https://lists.apache.org/thread/wb2821v85vn0lcbdzsbr8o672j8f53wj) on new Spark PRs (run by Claude, posted as plain comments that never approve anything), which at least puts the same tools on the defending side.

Scott also made the case for release management as a way into a community; it's stressful and mostly invisible (Russell Spitzer's reply to Neelesh on the 1.12 result thread was that he knew this one was a slog), and pressing the button at the end is apparently a real joy. Elsewhere, Unity Catalog's native Iceberg REST support, which Scott said was heading into a release candidate, arrived in [Unity Catalog 0.7.0](https://github.com/unitycatalog/unitycatalog/releases/tag/v0.7.0) on October 13.

Going back to the clock cells: the rhythm your body expresses was never a property of any single cell, only of how well they agree, and I think the same is true of a lakehouse. Iceberg's Spark runtime trailing Spark by a release, delta-rs waiting on DataFusion waiting on Arrow, and an enterprise that manages one big migration a decade are all the drift, and the week's proposals (a policy API the optimizer can't erase, a credential that refreshes itself, a gateway in front of the servers, a schema written in JSON) are the coupling that keeps the drift from showing up as a broken job. AI will keep making each clock run faster; what I don't know yet is whether it will also get good at the unglamorous work of keeping them in phase, which right now still falls on a small number of release managers and on the rest of us who have to upgrade.
