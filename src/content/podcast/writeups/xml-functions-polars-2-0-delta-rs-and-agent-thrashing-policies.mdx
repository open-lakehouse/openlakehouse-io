This is my write-up of episode 3 of This Week in Open Lakehouse, the weekly show Scott Haines and I host on open source data engineering news; the video is above, and the audio, show notes and full transcript are further down this page. We recorded this one in Paris on Thursday, September 24 (the thirty-ninth week of the year, which we established at exhausting length in the preamble), the morning after our Open Lakehouse + AI meetup, where I had the strange experience of having to kick people out of an office building at 10 PM on a Wednesday because the huddles would not break up.

Scott's framing of the week, once we got to it, was that almost everything under the open lakehouse umbrella is in a phase of solidification and hardening. I agreed, and added my own guess that the formats are getting so much attention because people are realizing how punishing it is to carry several of them. Reading the threads afterwards, I think we were describing the same thing from different ends.

**Nearly everything that moved this week was a project deciding what it would stop carrying (new equality deletes in Iceberg v4, StateFun and long-deprecated config keys in Flink, macOS Intel in Arrow, the in-memory default in Polars, `DeltaTableConfig` in delta-rs), and in almost every case the winning argument was what the project can still afford to test and maintain rather than how many people still use the thing; the agent half of the show was the same discipline applied to agents, where the expensive failure isn't a wrong attempt but an agent that keeps paying to retry one.**

## Iceberg 1.12, and the end of new equality deletes

Iceberg 1.12 was in its release candidate cycle when we recorded (Scott guessed a week to a week and a half, and 1.12.0 was [announced on October 1](https://lists.apache.org/thread/kst8b58yjnvfy353g2ysvb4fkx3xdcr9) after one more candidate). I also corrected myself live: v4 is not Parquet-only, and ORC and Avro are still in the spec.

The more consequential item was the vote on [forbidding new equality deletes in v4](https://lists.apache.org/thread/2pgfvf8v5ob221q5t2xwzjo4xvw5ltz1). Equality deletes record a delete as "remove any row matching these column values," which is cheap for a streaming writer and expensive for every reader afterwards, since each scan has to join data files against the delete files; it's the performance bottleneck I mentioned on the show. The community had already voted on the direction in July, and this vote was on the spec wording: v4 writers must not write new equality deletes, readers still apply existing ones from v2 and v3 tables, and upgrading stays a metadata-only operation. The [spec PR merged](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/17783) on September 29. This is the cleanest example of the week's pattern, because the old behavior isn't broken by removing it; the readers keep their obligation to old tables, and only the writing path, which pushed its costs onto everyone downstream, goes away.

Two smaller Iceberg items. One correction to what we said: Scott described a standard user agent "for the Rust clients," and the proposal is actually for REST clients generally (a header like `Spark/4.0.0 iceberg-spark/1.9.0 iceberg-java/1.9.0`, most specific first, and explicitly advisory, so servers must not reject requests over it). Scott's point about why it matters is the right one: it's how a catalog finds out which engines and clients actually call it, which is the evidence you need before you can say "no one uses this, let's get rid of it." The vote was reopened and [passed on October 9](https://lists.apache.org/thread/pllz983cvffryzcwskglsr0cyo7p0gxx) with 10 binding +1s. And on the C++ side, Scott called iceberg-cpp an early project "on the 0.4 release"; more precisely, its maintainers [proposed starting 0.4.0](https://lists.apache.org/thread/yfjj2p96w4s0fd7067fkr5dgknwp62d1) that week.

## Polaris does the housekeeping

I described the Polaris threads on the show as hygiene, which is most of a catalog's job after setup. The [soft-delete proposal](https://lists.apache.org/thread/1djyz0x3k7m71d71bqhw11c3syyshtdr) adds an opt-in, default-off hold period and undrop, for operators who need a recoverable drop (legal hold, a Nessie migration) and currently invent workarounds that REST clients can bypass; the dropped table's name stays reserved during the hold, so a legal-hold table never shares an identifier with a new live one.

The [page size thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/mmqllcbt2lfsrq571cfwbymgfwoxqdf0) is the one I'd point people to, because it's exactly the kind of interoperability edge Scott was talking about with URL encoding between catalogs. Polaris wants a server-side maximum on list page sizes so a single request can't produce an unbounded response; the Iceberg REST spec, however, says a server must return everything in one response when the client didn't send a page token, and old clients that don't paginate would silently report a truncated listing as complete. A protection that quietly breaks the oldest clients is precisely the trade a spec exists to make visible.

One correction: I described the cascading deletion discussion as cascading deletes of catalog objects, and the [thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/vpfq968ptnnr968ztb51w4tlo70mwqdn) is narrower than that. It's about Polaris's own persistence schema upgrades, and whether a migration that drops a table should also list out the index and primary key that a `DROP` removes by cascade anyway (easier to revert, more tedious to write). Scott's foreign-key explanation on the show is actually the closer description of it.

Governance got one more item on the Spark side, which Scott flagged in the opening as the one he was most stoked about: Huaxin Gao opened a [SPIP for pluggable row-filter and column-mask enforcement](https://lists.apache.org/thread/bfdvfq7576w64kmjhhtxf16xwd3rx041), a public DataSource V2 API through which a table or catalog declares readable columns, a row filter and column masks that Spark enforces during analysis. The motivation fits the week oddly well; today integrators like Ranger (via Kyuubi) inject rules through internal Catalyst APIs, and a masking projection added that way can be optimized away, silently returning unmasked data, so the proposal is as much about retiring a brittle workaround as adding a feature. (Scott also mentioned native CDC being debated in Spark; I couldn't find that thread, so I'm leaving it out until I can.)

## Flink cleans house, and XML comes back as a variant

This was our first time covering Flink (with my disclaimer that I'm not a streaming engineer). The cleanup threads are all subtraction: [FLIP-569](https://lists.apache.org/thread/ohpmyh7ovzjw8m49psy9hk4cg2hmyl3f) proposes sunsetting the Stateful Functions (StateFun) subproject, [FLIP-607](https://lists.apache.org/thread/4o6kd2fo0ndh9tqxdfvj5stovn3dzpv9) to migrate off the end-of-life OkHttp library was accepted on September 21, and a [discussion on removing long-deprecated configuration keys](https://lists.apache.org/thread/2o8s2lszhz6875v6oxpdrwn9hzw1ozm8) argues that keeping compatibility aliases indefinitely raises maintenance and testing costs and makes the configuration harder to understand. On the show I wondered aloud why StateFun was going, guessing that a contributor's great idea had outlived its maintainers, and the vote thread more or less confirms it; one reply put it as "the reason we'd sunset StateFun is that nobody is working on it anymore."

The addition of the week was [FLIP-612](https://lists.apache.org/thread/fpzc746vn5ko0cn2g25hvtrfd6b038dj), native XML functions for Flink SQL. It proposes `PARSE_XML` and `TRY_PARSE_XML` to turn an XML string into a `VARIANT`, and `XML_STRING` to turn it back, mirroring the existing `PARSE_JSON`, `TRY_PARSE_JSON` and `JSON_STRING`; once XML is a variant you query it with the same accessors and casts as parsed JSON. The hard part is the mapping (attributes and text go into `@` and `$` fields, and since variant objects sort fields by key, a `#` field recovers document order between differently named siblings), and the proposal is careful to note it needs no new SQL grammar. Even the one addition, in other words, is built by reusing machinery the project already maintains.

We both did a double take at XML showing up at all. Scott made the case for XML as the original structured format with real schemas (DTDs), still alive in banking. My angle was agents: XML is quietly being used far more by agents than by humans at this point, as the structure they're prompted and parsed with, which is a strange afterlife for a format most of us associated with front end development. Scott, who spent time at Buf, made the point I keep thinking about; agents lean on text and ripgrep, so binary structured data has to be decoded back to text to be searchable, and every round trip from typed objects to text and back costs something at scale.

## Polars 2.0 and delta-rs ship the breaking changes

[Polars 2.0](https://pola.rs/posts/announcing-polars-2/) was in pre-release when we recorded (the first release candidate went out September 2, and [2.0.0 shipped October 6](https://github.com/pola-rs/polars/releases/tag/py-2.0.0)). The headline is the one I read out on the show: calling `collect` on a `LazyFrame` now defaults to the streaming engine, which the Polars team expects to be "easily 5x faster" in aggregate. The cost is that the streaming engine doesn't guarantee row order for joins, group-bys and unpivots unless you ask for it with `maintain_order`, and the [upgrade guide](https://docs.pola.rs/releases/upgrade/2/) lists the other breaks (stricter `is_in`, removed casts, removed APIs) plus the Map change Scott liked, where Map columns from Arrow, Parquet and Iceberg now load as a proper `Map` type with Python `dict` values instead of lists of key/value structs. Scott's explanation of lazy frames is worth keeping (a four gigabyte file on a machine with one gigabyte of memory needs something that traverses it without loading it eagerly), and making streaming the default is Polars deciding that should be the normal case; the in-memory engine is still one setting away, it's just no longer what everyone gets for free.

For delta-rs, Scott had called the Rust 1.0 release early on a previous episode, and this week it actually happened: `deltalake` 1.0.0 went out on crates.io on September 21, with the [GitHub release](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/releases/tag/rust-v1.0.0) following on the 28th, removing the S3DynamoDbLogStore since it's no longer needed. One correction to his prediction that 2.0 would follow about a week later because any breaking change now forces a new major: as of this writing the crate has gone to 1.1.0, 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 instead. The Python package shipped [1.6.4](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/releases/tag/python-v1.6.4), [1.6.5](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/releases/tag/python-v1.6.5) and [1.6.6](https://github.com/delta-io/delta-rs/releases/tag/python-v1.6.6) in the same week (the metalhead in Scott is waiting for 6.6.6), with fixes for vacuum on multi-level partitions, partition detection (a directory only counts when its name is followed by `=`) and a CDF merge regression.

Scott's advice was to skip straight to 1.6.6, and I agree, but one part of what he said needs tightening: he said nothing in the upgrade will break, since you're opting into correctness. The correctness part is true; the releases also remove things, though. `DeltaTableConfig` was marked deprecated in 1.6.5 and removed outright in 1.6.6, and 1.6.4 replaced `PartitionFilter` with kernel predicates and dropped `file_pruning_predicate` from the Python read methods, so if your code touches any of those, read the notes before you bump. Scott also gave a shout-out to the maintainers, R. Tyler Croy and Ion Koutsouris among them, and to Tyler's habit of greeting the Delta Slack at 5:15 in the morning.

## Parquet's FastLanes thread and Arrow's Intel Macs

I described the [FastLanes thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/94q6oh41sxjlkrhhhwn09c66nmtm1xt9) on the show as a benchmark of FastLanes against Parquet, and that undersells it, so here's the correction. The study breaks FastLanes into its separate ideas and asks which ones Parquet should adopt: for plain PFOR, the lane-interleaved layout gave only a minor speedup on the authors' machine because Parquet's C++ reader is already vectorized, and the gains shrank further in multi-core tests where CPUs compete for memory bandwidth; for delta encoding, the transposed layout gave a large decoding speedup with almost no change in file size. Antoine Pitrou's reply is the most on-theme message of the week: he sketched how the sequential dependency in the existing `DELTA_BINARY_PACKED` decoder could be broken up, and argued that speeding up an encoding that's already everywhere beats introducing a new one "that people won't use before years."

Arrow's [macOS Intel thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/l3vmknbjhwf60crrfp3hwb7p5m4qjw7z) is the purest case of maintenance deciding support. The proposal exists because Homebrew stopped building Intel bottles and Arrow can no longer test some setups in CI. Antoine (whom I called Anton on the show, sorry) found that around 11% of macOS users in the Steam hardware survey still have Intel CPUs, which surprised both of us, but Kou's PyPI numbers from the same thread put macOS x86_64 wheels at about 0.1% of PyArrow downloads over the preceding thirty days, and Antoine himself agreed that dropping support makes sense if CI can't reasonably run. And yes, Wes McKinney replied that he has an Intel Mac Pro, for testing purposes, and offered to help test commits; we briefly judged him for it on air before reading the rest of the sentence.

## Agents, sandboxes and thrash detection

[Omnigent 0.15.0](https://github.com/omnigent-ai/omnigent/releases/tag/v0.15.0) came out the day we recorded, with Devin running as a native harness alongside Claude Code and Codex, and curated model catalogs for managed Kubernetes and Agent Sandbox sessions. The Kubernetes pattern is the part I care about; orchestrating thousands of stateful agent sessions in sandboxes is a different problem from the shared UI most people know Omnigent for. Scott's point was that nobody talks about bounding what an agent does outside its LLM calls (this sandbox gets so much RAM, so much local disk). Right now an agent running in my home directory with permissions bypassed can use whatever memory and disk it likes, and nothing on my side manages it.

That connects to a side project I described on the show: having agents write the same Spark pipelines many times over, comparing declarative syntax (Spark Declarative Pipelines) with plain imperative Spark code. It's still in progress, so take this as an observation rather than a finding, but the trade-off I keep seeing is compute against tokens. The declarative runs can iterate against a dry run without spending cluster compute, and the agent tends to iterate on the one function it's scoped to; the imperative runs varied wildly in the logic they used to reach the same end state (some brute-forced it with loops, some copied an answer from the internet) and would introduce random configurations across a whole pipeline file, and every attempt went through `spark-submit` to succeed or fail on real resources. Scott added the production version, where without a dry run a failing job pulls a terabyte into executors and retries each task four times before the stage fails.

Which is why my favorite Omnigent feature this week was the one that interrupted me. The built-in `detect_thrashing` policy (in my Databricks Omnigent workspace it shows up as Detect Agent Thrashing) caught an agent failing against a task it couldn't get past and alerted me, and I got to step in and steer it. The [mechanism](https://github.com/omnigent-ai/omnigent/blob/main/omnigent/policies/builtins/context.py) is simple: it watches tool results, and by default fires when the last five are all errors or when at least 80% of the last ten are, then asks the user to intervene; it deliberately doesn't reset after firing, since an agent that's thrashing will likely keep thrashing. My advice on the show stands: whatever you run your agents in, make sure it has thrash detection (also, as Scott noted, a great word for two metalheads).

## Come fact-check us

We closed on an invitation: if you're a committer on one of these projects and we misrepresented your work, come to one of our events and correct us in person (the Luma calendar is linked at the bottom of openlakehouse.io), and Scott admitted that every re-listen turns up something he said confidently that wasn't quite right. There's a fair amount of that in this post, including my own prediction that Spark 4.3 would land the following week; it was [announced on November 5](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jp456tktqwdns6gs5jv506b08c6n4f1t).

I think that's the same habit the week was about. Iceberg stopping new equality deletes, Flink letting StateFun go, Arrow conceding that it can't test Intel Macs anymore, and an agent being told to stop after its fifth failed tool call are all versions of noticing that carrying on costs more than stopping, and then saying so in writing where someone can check it. The meetup that wouldn't leave at 10 PM is the part of the ecosystem that makes that possible; the projects are small enough that the people who maintain them are the people in the room, arguing about metric view rollbacks until someone has to turn the lights off.
