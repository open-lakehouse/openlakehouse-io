This is my write-up of episode 7 of This Week in Open Lakehouse, the weekly show Scott Haines and I host on open source data engineering news; the video is above, and the audio, show notes and full transcript are further down this page. We recorded it back to back with the previous episode, right after I got back to the Bay from Glasgow, so if you watch them in a row and think I'm wearing the exact same thing, I am, and I'd rather you assume poor hygiene than ask.

I warned at the top that after a string of episodes where we talked endlessly about types in open table formats, this one would be engine-heavy, and it was. By the time I got to OpenCode 2.0 near the end, I said out loud that its new architecture was very similar to Spark Connect and very similar to Quack, and that this is just the world we live in now, client-server everything, with a real risk of wandering back into microservices swampy hell (I was trying not to swear at that point, having already gotten the podcast labeled explicit). I think the quip was more accurate than I meant it to be.

**Nearly everything that moved this week was something that used to live inside one process or one project being split across a boundary (DuckDB into a client and a server, OpenCode into a background service, Spark's database reads into Arrow batches instead of rows, Comet out of DataFusion and StreamFusion possibly into Flink), and the hard part in every case was deciding what is allowed to cross that boundary and who checks it, which is why the week's security fixes (a KMS URL read out of a Parquet file, cached privileges, unvalidated hosts) belong to the same story rather than a separate one.**

## DuckDB 2.0, in which a duck learns to listen on a port

DuckDB 2.0.0 LTS [shipped on October 21](https://duckdb.org/2026/10/21/announcing-duckdb-200), supported until at least October 2027, and it's the first major release since Amazon [agreed to acquire DuckLabs](https://aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/aws-ducklabs), the company behind it (the project stays independent under the DuckDB Foundation, MIT licensed). One correction: I had the codename backwards on the show. The official name is "Cinnamon Teal," the common name of *Spatula cyanoptera*, a reddish-brown duck of the western Americas; Cyanoptera is the species, not the release.

The headline is that `quack`, DuckDB's native client-server protocol, is now stable. A server calls `quack_serve`, a client attaches it and runs `CONNECT`, and the session points at the remote database in one statement; `CONNECT` also works against PostgreSQL and MySQL, with the optimizer pushing queries to the remote server instead of pulling tables over the wire. The server listens only on `localhost` by default, and the docs recommend a TLS-terminating reverse proxy over exposing it directly, which is the first of several times this week a project decided what may cross a boundary before anything else. Around it: asynchronous I/O for Parquet, CSV and native storage, Delta table creation and time travel, new Iceberg REST catalog `ATTACH` options, a DuckLake 1.1 specification, and `CREATE EXTENSION REPOSITORY` for your own signed extensions.

Scott's favorite part was Quack. Distributed systems usually start from a fixed shape (a Spark driver and its executors, Kafka moving from ZooKeeper to Raft just to agree on who leads), and the long-running dream is a self-assembling cluster where one instance connects to another and starts sharing load; DuckDB getting there with one statement is, as he put it, something that didn't have to be that easy.

My hot take was the breaking changes, the kind that bite whoever upgrades on release day. Single-arrow lambdas (`x -> x + 1`) are off by default in favor of `lambda x: x + 1` (which you should have been using already, and is an easy refactor to hand an agent), building needs a C++17 compiler, and database files created by 2.0 can't be opened by earlier versions. To be more precise than I was on the show: 2.0 still reads and writes your existing files, and those stay readable by older versions; only files 2.0 creates are one-way. The release post says to test on a shadow system first, which I'd take literally.

## Catalogs, or the business of saying no correctly

Scott ran the catalog news with a sore throat. A catalog exists to decide who crosses into the data, so its bugs are mostly yeses that should have been noes.

Unity Catalog merged `server.authorization.strict-privilege-checks` [on October 21](https://github.com/unitycatalog/unitycatalog/pull/2023), on by default. One correction to how we described it: Scott framed it as every permission check going back to the database with no caching, but the setting actually gates a set of corrected permission rules (rejecting grants on invalid object types, [checking read access before returning Iceberg credentials](https://github.com/unitycatalog/unitycatalog/pull/2015), requiring schema creation permission for model and volume renames), with `false` keeping the old, looser rules while deployments fix their grants. His example (boot me from a team and my next call should fail) is the right motivation; the mechanism is about which privilege a call requires, not a cache. Blocking catalog work also [moved off Armeria's event loops](https://github.com/unitycatalog/unitycatalog/pull/1918), since on HTTP/2 one slow database call could stall every stream on the connection.

Lakekeeper added [distributed cache invalidation](https://github.com/lakekeeper/lakekeeper/pull/2157), so when several instances share a database a change reaches all their caches within about a second, where before a removed role assignment could keep working until the cache's time-to-live ran out (up to two minutes, per the PR). Scott expected it in a 0.14.2 release; it shipped in [0.15.0](https://github.com/lakekeeper/lakekeeper/releases/tag/v0.15.0) on November 6. Polaris [integrated Paimon without delegation mode](https://github.com/apache/polaris/pull/3820) on the way to [1.9.0](https://lists.apache.org/thread/vvn28qykkfbvj07dhk5svnld532rjnty), with Jean-Baptiste Onofré driving the release, and the [Iceberg 1.12 adaptation](https://github.com/apache/polaris/pull/5360) Scott described as an open PR had already merged on October 7, which only helps his point that every catalog moves with the Iceberg REST spec in lockstep.

Paimon released its [first Terraform and OpenTofu provider](https://github.com/apache/terraform-provider-paimon) for its REST catalog, covering databases, tables, permissions, row filters and column masks, so access policy lives in code.

## Iceberg v4 keeps deciding what a file is

The File type is formally in the Iceberg v4 spec; the [vote passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/6b63cbcsf0l69z9hs6v9nc1gp4y96tlp) with 8 binding +1s and no objections, and the [spec PR merged](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/17918) on October 19. Scott's caveat was that merged into an unfinished spec isn't the same as part of the format (it can still come out if it proves wrong), and his real question was the one the type has to answer: is a file the contents of a blob column or a pointer that might later point at nothing?

I tied it to v4's multimodal direction: the file format API that puts one translation layer in front of Parquet, Avro and ORC (with an open question about Vortex or Lance), efficient column updates so recomputing one feature column doesn't rewrite every row, and single-file commits so a small write doesn't drag its own manifests along. Scott, who believed streaming solved everything until he worked on Iceberg, noted that Bufstream (from his time at Buf) and Delta's UniForm both coped with small writes by materializing the Iceberg table at the last moment; v4 is the attempt to make that trick unnecessary.

The [adaptive metadata tree spec merged](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/16025), so v4 manifests are Parquet rather than Avro, and in the [Parquet-only manifests thread](https://lists.apache.org/thread/l703q6mcyzbgkfczs91mj5bffk1k5pj4) Kevin Liu has now proposed dropping ORC and Avro as v4 data formats too, citing v3 feature gaps and thin non-Java support, which collides with the file format API's whole premise of choosing among formats. And a follow-up: the [User-Agent vote passed](https://lists.apache.org/thread/pllz983cvffryzcwskglsr0cyo7p0gxx) with 10 binding +1s. One correction, since we talked about it as a Rust thing: it recommends a standard User-Agent format for all REST clients, and the [PR](https://github.com/apache/iceberg/pull/17727) merged on November 10. Scott's reason it matters is the boundary argument in small: a catalog can't retire an old API version until the last laggard client moves, and the header tells you who that is.

## Arrow trusted a URL it found in a file

Scott covered [CVE-2026-92287](https://lists.apache.org/thread/0t6vry0msbk9dpsd0xv395ov5b84hj57), the cleanest example of the thesis I could have asked for. When Arrow C++ or PyArrow reads an encrypted Parquet file through a pluggable `KmsClient` and the application hasn't set the KMS URL, the URL written in the file gets forwarded to the client, and a client that doesn't validate hosts sends its KMS token wherever the file's author pointed it. It affects Arrow C++ from 4.0.0 and PyArrow from 8.0.0 up to 26.0.0, which [shipped on October 19](https://lists.apache.org/thread/rxzv8v89lcq0wbxmr1qdj573dgfclogz) and makes file-controlled KMS URLs opt-in through a `read_kms_url` flag; if you can't upgrade, set the URL in `KmsConnectionConfig` or validate hosts in your client.

## DataFusion's family moves house

Comet voted to leave DataFusion and become Apache Comet. Andy Grove's [proposal](https://lists.apache.org/thread/0szsfkrog2jjr2x92zl78201zh131qzq) argues that unlike DataFusion's other subprojects, which extend DataFusion, Comet is a Spark accelerator that uses DataFusion as its engine, with a contributor community that is now largely distinct; the [proposal document](https://github.com/apache/datafusion-comet/blob/a230ae54d223e0b60097c12e470a87d54e802b0e/docs/source/about/top_level_project_proposal.md) puts Comet at about 1.8 times faster than Spark 4.2 on TPC-DS at scale factor 1000. Two corrections: we discussed the vote as open, but it had [passed on October 23](https://lists.apache.org/thread/5mpcspj7t5kwht3yqmpmzd3j2xnp1b9l) with twenty-five +1s (eight binding), and the resolution now waits for the December 16 board meeting; and the cap of three open PRs for contributors without write access I called likely had [already merged](https://github.com/apache/datafusion-comet/pull/7135) on October 20, because review capacity is the bottleneck and agents make opening PRs nearly free.

Going the other way, StreamFusion, a Flink-native engine built on DataFusion and living in `datafusion-contrib`, was [proposed on the Flink dev list](https://lists.apache.org/thread/jc9j6r21fjm0qf75dowgfny89vv4wqp7) as a Flink subproject. As someone who likes organization (when I'm good at it), I find it frustrating to watch projects move without standard reasons, since users have to work out where things live now. Scott was more sympathetic; DataFusion was meant to be the Swiss Army knife of query engines, so a DataFusion layer for Flink SQL living under Flink is the natural shape. I think we're both right, which is the annoying thing about governance; each move makes sense locally and the map gets harder to read.

## Spark wants Arrow at the door, and lookups on demand

The [ADBC data source SPIP](https://lists.apache.org/thread/vz0s8nok4mgyfmcz1wmoc9ko14xkts9o), from Tornike Gurgenidze with Holden Karau shepherding, proposes a built-in `adbc` format beside JDBC rather than replacing it. JDBC hands Spark a row at a time and Spark reads each value with its own call, so a columnar database's columns become rows and then, if Comet or Gluten is involved, columns again; ADBC drivers return Arrow record batches Spark can wrap as `ColumnarBatch` almost without copying. On the [prototype](https://github.com/tokoko/spark-adbc), reads from columnar databases run 3 to 10 times faster than JDBC, with the gap growing on wider tables, which as I said on the show is a prototype number to hold loosely.

| | JDBC source | Proposed ADBC source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What comes back | Rows, read value by value | Arrow record batches, wrapped almost without copying |
| Partitioning | Range partitioning on a column | Range partitioning, plus partitions planned by the driver |
| Database differences | Per-database dialect classes in Spark | Driver reports syntax through SqlInfo; filter values sent as bound Arrow parameters |
| Writes | Append, at-least-once | Append through bulk ingest, same guarantee; job-level atomic writes and Structured Streaming out of scope |

Scott tied this straight back to Quack: Spark Connect already exchanges Arrow, ADBC does the same for Arrow-native sources, and both are strict, schema'd binary formats, which from Scott is high praise in the same family as his love of protobuf. It matches what I see across Spark (more Arrow inside, more room for Rust tools); the Rust Spark Connect client I'm writing about hands its Arrow output straight to Polars or DuckDB.

The [External Lookup Join SPIP](https://lists.apache.org/thread/7s6tgy9cm18jnzykr8htrtrw4nxyrhp1) from Jungtaek Lim handles a different crossing. Every existing join strategy materializes the static side first, so a stream-static join reloads and prunes it every micro-batch even when the store behind it (an RDBMS, an in-memory store, NoSQL) is built for point lookups; the proposal queries the store on demand while processing the other side, with real-time mode benefiting most. Scott, who once opened a PR to bring the now-dead Spark Redis connector to Spark 4, put the appeal plainly: Redis can answer a million-key multi-get on demand, which doesn't fix orphaned rows but does remove the intermediate state. My take was that it brings Spark closer to Flink's lookup joins, and that between this and real-time mode Spark has spent the year catching up on streaming faster than its reputation. One correction: I called it two days old and very early, but discussion opened on October 7 and the vote [passed on October 23](https://lists.apache.org/thread/678vpc4x475sjsn2lmfhwbdd4r0cxrjc) with 10 +1s (6 binding) and no -1s.

## Webhooks, and two agent tools

Airflow's [AIP-125: Webhooks](https://lists.apache.org/thread/l6yy8mspb1lls5qhg9h910rd0rc55nly) gives Airflow a `POST /webhooks/<name>` endpoint where each sender has a shared secret in a connection; Airflow verifies the signature, records the delivery and acts on it (start a Dag, wake a waiting task without holding a worker slot, cancel a run), and the endpoint can run as its own process so it can face the internet without the UI or REST API attached. Scott compared it to Temporal's durable execution model and expects agents to do most of the triggering.

[OpenCode 2.0](https://anoma.ly/notes/opencode-2-0/) now runs its server as a single background process, with terminal, web and desktop clients all talking to it, and adds background subagents, Code Mode (a small JavaScript interpreter that turns MCP servers and OpenAPI endpoints into functions an agent's script calls in one round trip, instead of loading every tool into context) and hot-reloadable plugins the agent can write for itself mid-session. Scott closed with [Omnigent 0.19.0](https://github.com/omnigent-ai/omnigent/releases/tag/v0.19.0), whose MCP gateway puts managed MCP tools behind the server's policy checks and lets sandboxed agents use approved Git repositories over SSH without ever holding the key, and [0.19.1](https://github.com/omnigent-ai/omnigent/releases/tag/v0.19.1), a single change to stop trusting the compromised eMudhra intermediate CA.

## Shout-outs, and the swamp

We closed with new committers and PMC members: [Arnav Balyan](https://lists.apache.org/thread/ytdob31cd38cz6j3mdq2bm1bzwjco80c) and [Chaoyang Liu](https://lists.apache.org/thread/om2o9q1zdkxzpxylfo96ohhlxsy2scqh) in Paimon, [Ramin Gharib](https://lists.apache.org/thread/l89sryydod2dmcb37x28rt8oyyp8hl5r) in Flink, [Richard Baah](https://lists.apache.org/thread/tj66yqr9shrrzl6qo07bm55cxdw7778s) in Arrow, and Gang Wu on the Iceberg PMC (the [ASF roster](https://whimsy.apache.org/public/committee-info.json) dates it to September 16). Congratulations to all of them, and if we missed you, tell us and we'll fix it next week.

I keep coming back to my microservices line, because I don't think it was only a joke. Splitting a thing in two is easy; the swamp was never the split, it was all the implicit trust that used to live inside one process suddenly becoming traffic across a wire that nobody had decided how to check. What I find encouraging about this week is that most of the projects did the deciding first: Quack listens on localhost until you put a proxy in front of it, ADBC sends filter values as bound parameters instead of literals, Airflow's webhook endpoint can run without the rest of Airflow, and the one real vulnerability of the week was precisely a boundary nobody had drawn (a URL from inside a file, treated as though the application had written it). If the next few years are client-server everything, and I suspect they are, the projects that stay out of the swamp will be the ones that treat the check at the boundary as the feature rather than the paperwork.
