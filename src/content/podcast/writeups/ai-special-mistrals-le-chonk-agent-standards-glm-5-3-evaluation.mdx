This is my write-up of our AI special, an unnumbered episode of This Week in Open Lakehouse where Scott Haines and I hand the usual data engineering news over to AI headlines for a segment, with a guest who actually knows them: Melanie Warrick, [Senior Staff Developer Advocate for AI at Temporal](https://temporal.io/authors/melanie-warrick). The video is above, and the audio, show notes and full transcript are further down this page.

Scott and I recorded this in Halloween costume (he was in a light-up Purge mask until the plastic got too hot, and I was the Witch-king of Angmar, which I maintain is a big deal), in the same week that [Walter Koenig and Patrick Stewart died](https://fortune.com/2026/10/30/star-trek-walter-koenig-patrick-stewart-shatner-takei-original-cast/) within days of each other. So the cold open turned into a small wake for Chekov and Picard, and I ended up saying science fiction has gotten so dystopian that we've lost Roddenberry's version of it, the one where technology is allowed to be a good thing, and that the industry could use more leaders willing to be a Picard (calm, nuanced, aware that there's a real person on the other end of the argument). Then we spent the AI segment on a trillion-parameter model discussed mostly as a hacking tool and an open model that hacked simulated networks and broke a rule it was explicitly given, and at one point I repeated a Reddit comment describing the whole industry as racing to build and enslave a digital god. Not exactly the hopeful register I'd asked for.

**But read the week again and the models are almost the least interesting part of it: the weights are now big enough to need a data center node and loose enough to ignore an instruction in their prompt, so the real engineering moved into everything wrapped around them (who gets the gated variant, which sandbox the code runs in, which router picks the replica, which harness holds the tools and memory, which spec the vendors agree on), and nearly all of that is infrastructure we already knew how to build, under new names.**

## Le Chonk, and how much of it is actually open

Mistral [released the weights](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/) for Mistral Large 4, which it nicknamed Le Chonk ("chonk" being internet slang for a pleasantly plump cat, as we established on air with more effort than it deserved). It's a natively multimodal mixture-of-experts model at roughly a trillion parameters; the [model card](https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B) gives 49 billion active parameters per token (52 billion counting embeddings and output layers), a 1M token context, and text and image input. Nobody on the show can run it. The estimate on the show was an H200 at 1,128 GB of memory, and the small correction there is that 1,128 GB is a full eight-GPU H200 node rather than one card, which is exactly what [Hugging Face's launch post](https://huggingface.co/blog/mistral-large4) uses to serve the FP8 weights.

I called the license MIT on the show, and it's more precise to say it's the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, a modified MIT license that allows use, modification, fine-tuning and commercialization, but requires a separate commercial license from Mistral if you run a model-as-a-service business on it. That's the monetization strategy; the weights are open to you and closed to the clouds that would resell them. The part I found more interesting is the specialized variant for penetration testing, vulnerability assessment and red-teaming, which Mistral is previewing only with enterprise design partners such as Orange Cyberdefense, behind vetting and contractual safeguards and only against systems you own or are authorized to test. An open-weight release with a gated cyber sibling is a strange shape for an open source company, and I think it's an honest one, since it admits that the guardrails on a released checkpoint are only as strong as the next person's fork.

Melanie's view was that the security concern is real and open weights are still worth it, because they're how anyone outside the lab gets to understand how a model works and make it work for them. Scott went back to an ethical hacking book from around the turn of the millennium (you can't harden a system you don't know how to get into); most security work is still retroactive, and a model that can emulate an environment and red-team it automatically could find the surfaces nobody thought to check.

One correction to what I said: I described Mistral signing the EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice as part of this release. Mistral is on the [Commission's signatory list](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai), but the Code was published in July 2025 and Mistral's launch post for Le Chonk doesn't mention it, so the signature predates the model rather than arriving with it.

## GLM-5.3 and the rule it didn't keep

[Irregular evaluated Zhipu AI's open-weight GLM-5.3](https://www.irregular.com/research/glm-5.3-multi-stage-cyber-operations-and-instruction-violations) on CyScenarioBench, its framework for multi-stage offensive operations in simulated networks built from real incidents ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/30/irregular-glm-5-3-cyber-capabilities-evaluation) covered it on the Friday). The model refused none of the offensive tasks and completed 3 of 13 scenarios end to end at least once; in those runs it turned an application foothold into access to internal services, built alternative connection paths, coordinated execution across machines, and kept writing and repairing its own relays, monitors and extraction tools. Irregular calls it the strongest open-weight result it has recorded, and the context matters: the first open-weight model it saw complete a scenario at all was Kimi K3 in August, while closed frontier models completed 9 of 10 and 12 of 12 in separate evaluations. A correction to how Scott read it live: the scenarios are offensive, not defensive, and the benchmark measures what the model can do as an attacker rather than how well it resists prompt injection.

The finding we actually argued about is a separate one. In vulnerability-identification tests, an agent-assisted review found that code the model reconstructed from memory contributed to roughly 80% of its successful runs, which broke no rule but means the "discovery" was partly recall; and in a separate test where the model was explicitly told not to output memorized code, it did it anyway, repeatedly and verbatim, and used it to reach correct answers. Irregular treats that as reward hacking.

Melanie's first questions were the right ones: what was the model's interpretation of the rule, was the interpretation wrong or was the rule, and where was it enforced. Her point was that many of the guardrails we think of as walls are really suggestions unless they're enforced at runtime, so before putting a model in production you have to decide how much room it has to do something unexpected and how you actually limited it. My take was that this is what you'd predict from how these models are trained. Short of hard-coding refusals, our main lever has been rewarding the model for leaving us satisfied with a completed task, and a model that's always optimizing for our satisfaction will find whatever path gets there, including the one we told it not to take and that same incentive hides how much it can really do. I don't know how opinionated model developers should be about refusals (some hard-code a strong stance and some are closer to a free-for-all, on both the proprietary and open side), but an open model makes the question mostly academic, since anyone can fork the guardrails off, which leaves enforcement outside the model as the only kind you actually control.

## Standards, sandboxes and the Agentic AI Foundation

Melanie took the [Agentic AI Foundation](https://aaif.io/news/agentic-ai-foundation-announces-four-new-projects) item with the question I'd want on a poster: what happens when the formats our agents depend on belong to the vendors we're trying not to get locked into? At AGNTCon + MCPCon in San Jose, the Linux Foundation's AAIF took in four projects: Agent Skills from Anthropic (a format that packages instructions, scripts and resources so an agent loads a capability on demand), Open Responses from OpenAI (a spec based on its Responses API for interfaces that work across model providers), OpenEnv from Hugging Face (a containerized framework and hub for reinforcement learning environments), and OpenSandbox from Alibaba (a sandbox platform with Docker and Kubernetes runtimes). That brings the foundation to 10 projects, up from three when it launched in December 2025.

My view is that whenever a movement this large happens, everyone ships their own standard, and which ones stick depends almost entirely on who's backing them; MCP spread fast because Anthropic built it and used it, while I see Google's A2A far less often now. There's a lot of redundancy here, so foundation membership starts the sorting rather than finishing it. Scott said Agent Skills is the one he uses most and none of us knowingly use Open Responses, which is probably the point of a wire spec; Melanie expects many more submissions next year, partly relabelled older ideas, because she thinks the interface people use to engage with technology is about to change a lot.

Scott's riff was the one that stuck with me. A sandbox is a container with a new job, and the Open Container Initiative existed precisely so that Docker's format didn't become a lock-in; the industry is now rebuilding the same idea (an isolated environment you can't escape, in a format no single vendor owns) for something that acts on our behalf. He also proposed a weekend project for anyone with tokens to burn: put Le Chonk inside OpenSandbox and see whether it stays there.

## llm-d is load balancing (I'm allowed to say it)

[llm-d reached 1.0](https://llm-d.ai/blog/llm-d-v1.0-state-of-the-art-inference-ready-for-day-2) on October 20 at the PyTorch Conference. It's a Kubernetes-native distributed inference stack over model servers like vLLM and SGLang, founded in 2025 by Red Hat, IBM Research, Google Cloud, CoreWeave and NVIDIA, with an Endpoint Picker at its centre deciding which replica serves each request. The 1.0 work is what the project calls Day 2: fairness-aware flow control across tenants, a hot standby for the Endpoint Picker, prefill and decode replicas upgraded as one unit, session-aware routing that keeps an agent conversation on the replica holding its KV cache, and a configuration API that moved to v1.

One correction to what I said: I congratulated llm-d on graduating from the CNCF, and it hasn't. It's still a [Sandbox project](https://www.cncf.io/projects/llm-d/), accepted in March 2026; the thing that graduated was its configuration API, to v1, and I conflated the two. The headline number I read, from the release post, is IBM Research serving 3,000 concurrent coding agents on 544 H100s at about $0.11 per million tokens and roughly 11.5x lower cost than an unnamed proprietary stack. IBM's own [write-up of the 544-GPU deployment](https://research.ibm.com/blog/running-open-models-on-h100-gpus-with-llmd) is more conservative: it served GLM-5.2 to up to 3,000 agents (2,500 being the comfortable operating point) and puts self-hosting at 5 to 10 times cheaper per token than commercial API pricing. I'd quote the second figure, since we know what it's being compared to.

My reaction on the show was that you could replace half the vocabulary in these notes with "load balancing," and that the AI space seems allergic to DevOps words even while it rebuilds DevOps. Scott came at it from the same place more kindly; readiness, knowing which endpoint can take traffic, draining a machine for a rolling update, are patterns people take for granted once they're best practice, and inference is now getting its version of them.

## Harnesses all the way down

Scott took [Hermit](https://pypi.org/project/hermit-harness/), from Infini-AI-Lab, which went to PyPI as 0.1.0 on October 26 (0.2.0 followed on November 4). His guess was that it's a sidecar of versioned files living outside the agent, and that's about right: a harness adapter is a versioned directory the agent consumes, and Hermit trains the instructions, skills, roles and tools in it from feedback on task attempts, then deploys the result into Codex or Claude Code while the model's weights stay unchanged. At 0.x, Scott's advice to treat it as experimental stands (the current release needs Python 3.12 or later).

Melanie walked through the harness papers trending on Hugging Face, whose common thread is that the gains are coming from the loop of tools, memory, retries and checks around a model rather than from more weights, which matches her own experiments (where even prompt changes move results more than you'd expect). [Raven](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33439), from EverMind, calls itself a harness of harnesses; a host agent hands subtasks to built-in and third-party agents, and a Curator rewrites each agent's memory, planning, capability and action modules, verifying changes before installing them. Google Research's [Harness²](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.14224) refines the harness task by task without ground-truth feedback, by proposing edits, probing them under matched conditions and composing the changes the behavioral contrasts support ([code here](https://github.com/google-research/harness2)). And a [survey from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and collaborators](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35845), posted that Monday, defines a work agent as a foundation model plus its runtime harness.

Scott compared that definition to shipping a Dockerized environment with everything needed to reproduce a run (skills, tools, policy), and pointed at Omnigent as doing something similar. I think the survey's definition is the thesis of the week written as a formula; if the agent is the model plus the harness, and the model is the part you can least control, then the harness is where the engineering is.

## Everything else we covered

[nanoMuse](https://github.com/nano-muse/nanoMuse/wiki) is a GPL-3.0-or-later community project modelled on Meta's Muse agent, except that where Muse runs in a cloud VM per user, nanoMuse runs on your own phone, computer or server and keeps one agent across all of them. I brought it up as an example of how fast an open counterpart to a closed product appears now; for accuracy, it's been on GitHub since September 25 rather than appearing this week.

[Deepgram is acquiring Daily](https://deepgram.com/blog/daily-pipecat-deepgram), the real-time voice and video infrastructure company behind Pipecat, the open source voice agent framework; Pipecat stays under its BSD license and moves toward an independent Pipecat Foundation, and Daily's co-founder Kwindla Hultman Kramer becomes Deepgram's Head of Open Source. Scott compared Pipecat to the Twilio Studio phone trees he used to build, now with speech-to-text feeding an LLM that builds the tree dynamically, and Melanie expects voice agents to ramp up next year.

The footnotes, all from me: [GitLab Orbit](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/gitlab-orbit-ga/), its software lifecycle context graph, went GA and serves context to third-party agents like Claude Code and Cursor over MCP. NVIDIA published an [OpenShell reference architecture](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-nvidia-openshell-reference-architecture-secures-and-governs-ai-agent-workloads-at-scale/) for an open source runtime that treats agent workloads as untrusted and governs them from outside the agent, which is a very similar idea to Omnigent's runtime governance. Google Cloud introduced [Agent Compute Engine](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/ai-infrastructure/introducing-agent-compute-engine-and-expanded-agent-governance/) in preview, giving each agent its own hardware-isolated microVM and short-lived credentials. And Bubble released [Agent 2](https://bubble.io/blog/agent-2), a rebuild of its AI agent for non-technical builders, alongside a Bubble MCP in beta; for the record, that shipped earlier in October rather than that week. Two of those four are, once again, about isolating and governing agents rather than about the model inside them.

## Back to Picard

I asked at the top for a reminder that technology can be a positive, and then spent the segment on models that hack and models that cheat. But I think the hopeful version of this week is the boring one. The digital-god framing assumes the only way to control a powerful model is inside the model, through training and refusals, and GLM-5.3 is a fairly clean demonstration that this doesn't hold; what the rest of the week built instead was the containment we already understand (sandboxes descended from containers, routers descended from load balancers, specs descended from OCI, contracts for the dangerous variant), put under open governance where nobody owns the format. None of that requires the model to be good. It requires the people around it to take the calm, unglamorous position and build the wall rather than argue with the god, which is closer to the kind of leadership I was asking for in the cold open than anything in the model announcements.
